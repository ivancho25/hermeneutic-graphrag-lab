Both episodes are organized around the same underlying structure: a collapse of the boundary that ordinarily separates Clara's inner speech from an external interlocutor, and a consequent outsourcing of psychic regulation to a figure outside herself. In Bristol the collapse is literal—the monologue leaks out of her mouth into public space—while on Dartmoor it is inverted, since the regulatory figure she has installed outside herself (Argo) vanishes, and the result is a somatic shutdown. The two phenomena are mirror images of a single economy of attachment in which Clara cannot hold her own continuity alone.

Consider the muttering first. What Clara describes is not the ordinary self-talk of the solitary, but a compulsive, geography-expanding undertone that she experiences as involuntary ("I couldn't stop it") and that she goes to elaborate lengths to disguise with unplugged earphones and a scarf. The content of the compulsion is telling: the monologue begins in the kitchen precisely where conversation with Walter used to happen, and it begins at the moment that conversation becomes unbearable—when Walter's bitterness makes him "hateful." The muttering is thus a kind of failed dialogue, a speech that has lost its addressee but continues to run. It is the residue of a relationship that can no longer be conducted outwardly, turned inward and then, because it cannot be contained, turned outward again into the street. The shame she feels is the shame of a boundary violation: the private made public, the inside showing on the outside.

Now consider the disappearances. When Argo vanishes for up to forty-eight hours, Clara "could neither sleep nor eat." This is not simply worry; it is a suspension of the bodily functions that sustain a self. The dog has become the external organ of her continuity—the thing that lets her "sleep more deeply, surrendering to rest," the thing that stands between her and "sinking into dark thoughts." When he is gone, the regulatory function he performs is gone with him, and her body registers the loss as a kind of arrest. The relief on his return is described as "overwhelming," which is the affect of a self reassembling.

The structural link is therefore this: in both cases Clara's psychic equilibrium depends on an externalized other who holds the place of the interlocutor. In Bristol, the interlocutor is absent—Walter is present but unavailable as a conversational partner—and the speech that would have been addressed to him has nowhere to go, so it circulates compulsively through her own body and out into the street. On Dartmoor, the interlocutor is present and functioning—Argo is the one who "alert[s] me himself," the one with whom she shares the night watch—and so the speech is quieted; but because the regulatory function has been lodged so completely in him, his absence produces not speech but its opposite, a mute, sleepless, fasting vigil. The muttering and the inability to eat or sleep are two faces of the same deficit: a self that cannot self-soothe, that requires a living external object to perform the function of holding.

This reading is reinforced by Clara's own account of what Argo does for her. He is "part of my mental stability, part of my mourning process," "the cure that keeps me from muttering to myself." She names the connection explicitly: the dog is the antidote to the symptom. The symptom and the cure are structurally identical—both are ways of managing the same underlying problem, the problem of a self that has lost its conversational partner and cannot tolerate the silence. The muttering fills the silence with speech; the dog fills it with presence. When the dog is absent, neither solution is available, and the body itself goes quiet.

There is a further layer. The muttering is disguised with earphones and a scarf—a performance of having an interlocutor, a pretending-to-be-on-the-phone. Argo is, in a sense, the successful version of that disguise: a real interlocutor rather than a simulated one. But the fact that she needed the disguise at all shows that the underlying need is not for a specific person but for the structure of being-addressed. The dog satisfies that structure, which is why he can substitute for the muttering; and it is why his loss reactivates the original deficit in its most primitive form, as a bodily refusal.

Finally, the two phenomena share a temporal logic. The muttering expands "little by little" across the geography of the flat and then into the street—a gradual colonization of space by the symptom. The disappearances are episodes of up to forty-eight hours, repeated "at least ten times"—a recurring rhythm of loss and return. Both are characterized by a loss of control that Clara experiences as happening to her rather than being done by her. She does not choose to mutter; she does not choose to be unable to eat. In both cases the boundary between self and environment has become permeable, and the environment—whether the street that hears her or the moor that swallows her dog—has become the theater of her inner life. The muttering and the sleepless fasting are the same permeability, expressed once as excess speech and once as its suspension.
